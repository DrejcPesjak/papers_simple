One man’s case for building

Technology made
modern life.
So build. Faster.

In 2023 Marc Andreessen — one of the most powerful investors in Silicon Valley — wrote down what he thinks technology is for. His answer: it is the reason your life is comfortable, we have wrongly turned against it, and the right response is to build more of everything, as fast as we can. More AI. More nuclear power. Fewer people asking us to slow down.

It is a short, forceful piece of writing, and much of it is argued over. Here is what it says, step by step, in plain English — so you can work out which parts you buy.

One thing to know going in: this is a manifesto. It makes its case by stating it. There are no studies, no data and no footnotes anywhere in it.

The Techno-Optimist Manifesto by Marc Andreessen · a16z · 16 October 2023.
Read the original on a16z.com ↗
The whole argument, in six steps
  1. 01For almost all of human history, life barely changed at all.
  2. 02Then we started inventing things, and living standards shot up.
  3. 03Technology is why — and it is the only thing that can keep growth going.
  4. 04Free markets are what carry those gains out to everyone.
  5. 05Make intelligence and energy cheap enough and almost everything becomes cheap.
  6. 06So build, as fast as possible. Whoever slows this down is causing harm.
The one big idea

Everything hangs on one loop.

Underneath all the slogans there is one machine. The manifesto calls it the techno-capital machine: a circle where each step feeds the next, round and round, forever. Tap any part of it to see what the manifesto claims.

Pink = the loop. Green = the two fuels it runs on.

Step 01 of 04 · the loop

Technology

“We believe technology is a lever on the world – the way to make more with less.”

Better tools let people produce more from the same effort and materials. The manifesto treats this as the one source of growth that never runs out — unlike population, which is falling in rich countries, and raw resources, which are finite.

Tap a box in the diagram to move through the loop. The claim is that this circle has no ceiling: it just keeps spiralling upward.

The manifesto’s words
In plain terms
01 / Lies & Truth

The opening move

“We are being lied to.”
“We are told that technology takes our jobs, reduces our wages, increases inequality, threatens our health, ruins the environment… We are told to be pessimistic.”
”

It starts by telling you that you’ve been misled.

Before making any claim of its own, the manifesto establishes that the pessimistic view you may already hold was put there by someone else. Every worry about technology gets gathered into one bundle and labelled a campaign — including worries people arrived at on their own.

Then comes the reversal: technology is “the glory of human ambition,” and it’s time to “raise the technology flag” again.

What you’d have to acceptThat a mood explains most concerns about jobs, health and the environment, and that the specific things which happened to specific people explain very few of them.

02 / Technology

Growth or death

“Societies, like sharks, grow or die.”
“There are only three sources of growth: population growth, natural resource utilization, and technology… And so the only perpetual source of growth is technology.”
”

Growth is treated as a matter of survival.

The argument is a process of elimination. Populations in developed countries are shrinking. Natural resources have hard limits. That leaves technology as the only thing that can keep an economy expanding indefinitely — so anything that slows technology is, in this frame, choosing decline.

It goes further: “there is no material problem… that cannot be solved with more technology.” Starvation got the Green Revolution; darkness got electric light; pandemics got vaccines.

What you’d have to acceptThat a society which stops growing necessarily degrades and collapses — and that the list of past solved problems predicts the list of future ones.

03 / Markets

The discovery machine

“Markets are an inherently individualistic way to achieve superior collective outcomes.”
“A market sets wages as a function of the marginal productivity of the worker. Therefore technology – which raises productivity – drives wages up, not down.”
”

Markets are described as a form of intelligence.

Nobody at the centre knows enough to plan an economy — the useful information sits with the people actually buying and selling. Prices carry that information around. Competition punishes whoever gets it wrong. Self-interest is the fuel, and the manifesto is cheerful about that — “have you met people?”

The section makes the manifesto’s boldest economic promise: because technology raises what a worker can produce, it must raise what a worker is paid. It calls this “perhaps the most counterintuitive idea in all of economics.”

What you’d have to acceptThat gains from higher productivity reliably reach workers as wages. Economists actively disagree here — whether pay has tracked productivity over recent decades is one of the most contested questions in the field, and the manifesto doesn’t engage with the dispute.

04 / Jobs

Infinite wants

“Since human wants and needs are infinite, economic demand is infinite, and job growth can continue forever.”
“A Universal Basic Income would turn people into zoo animals to be farmed by the state.”
”

Automation can’t run out of work for people to do.

The reasoning: people never stop wanting things, so demand never stops, so there is always something new to be paid for. Technology widens the range of things humans can usefully do. Invoking Ricardo’s comparative advantage, the manifesto says high employment is guaranteed “regardless of the level of technology.”

That’s also why it rejects UBI outright. The objection is about dignity — being useful, in this telling, is part of what makes a person a person.

What you’d have to acceptThat the new work shows up quickly enough, and close enough to the people who lost the old work, for them to get through the change — and that it works out for those people, as well as for the economy as a whole.

05 / Intelligence

Making sand think

“We are literally making sand think.”
“We believe any deceleration of AI will cost lives. Deaths that were preventable by the AI that was prevented from existing is a form of murder.”
”

AI is framed as a universal problem solver — and slowing it down as a moral failure.

The claim is that intelligence is what drives every other improvement, so more of it, from any source, makes everything better. AI could cut deaths from disease, car crashes and more. Therefore delay has a body count.

This is the manifesto’s sharpest move. It takes the safety argument — go slower, we might cause harm — and flips the burden: the harm is in the waiting.

What you’d have to acceptThat a faster rollout’s saved lives belong on the ledger while the harms it might cause stay off it. The comparison only ever runs one way. Ten arguments for dismissing AI risk ↗ looks at the other side.

06 / Energy

Turn it up 1,000×

“We should raise everyone to the energy consumption level we have, then increase our energy 1,000x, then raise everyone else’s energy 1,000x as well.”
“We have the silver bullet for virtually unlimited zero-emissions energy today – nuclear fission.”
”

Use much more energy, and make it nuclear.

The manifesto refuses the trade-off between energy and the environment. Fission already exists and emits no carbon; fusion is coming. What stops us building a thousand reactors, it argues, is regulation and fear. The physics was settled decades ago — Nixon called for exactly that build-out in 1973, and it never happened.

It flips the green argument too: rich, technologically advanced countries clean up their environments, poor stagnant ones wreck them. The example given is the Aral Sea under the USSR.

What you’d have to acceptThat cost, construction time, waste and public consent will all yield to politics once the will is there — and that one comparison with the Soviet Union settles the general point about growth and the environment.

07 / Abundance

Everything as cheap as a pencil

“If we make both intelligence and energy ‘too cheap to meter’, the ultimate result will be that all physical goods become as cheap as pencils.”
“We believe the global population can quite easily expand to 50 billion people or more, and then far beyond that as we ultimately settle other planets.”
”

Cheap intelligence plus cheap energy makes everything else cheap.

Every price that falls is effectively a raise for everyone who buys that thing. Push enough prices toward zero and living standards explode. The pencil is the image: genuinely complex to manufacture, yet so cheap nobody minds losing one.

Then the loop closes. More abundance supports more people; people are “the ultimate resource,” because each one might be the next inventor; more people generate more abundance. Hence 50 billion, then the stars.

What you’d have to acceptThat the loop has no physical ceiling — no material, land, water or ecological limit that doesn’t itself yield to more technology.

08 / Values

Who you should become

“We are not victims, we are conquerors.”
“We believe in ambition, aggression, persistence, relentlessness – strength… We believe in risk, in leaps into the unknown… We believe in an absolute rejection of resentment.”
”

Halfway through, it stops describing the world and starts describing a character.

Building things is framed as a virtue — an adventure, a frontier, a hero’s journey. Readers are urged to become the kind of people who can advance technology: technically educated, hands-on, ambitious, unembarrassed about wanting to win.

It heads off the obvious charge of utopianism. Borrowing Thomas Sowell’s “constrained vision,” it says: take people as they are, test ideas in the real world, expect small gains that add up. The goal it settles on is “slouching toward Utopia.”

What you’d have to acceptThat a whole society should adopt these values, and not only the people who already hold them. This part of the document makes no economic claim, so there is no data against which to check it.

The list

“Our enemies are not bad people – but rather bad ideas.”

Thirteen sentences begin “Our enemy is…”, naming twelve targets. Read together, they show what the manifesto counts as an obstacle — and it’s a wide net.

Stagnation Anti-merit, anti-ambition, anti-greatness Statism, authoritarianism, collectivism, central planning, socialism Bureaucracy, vetocracy, gerontocracy, blind deference to tradition Corruption, regulatory capture, monopolies, cartels Corroded institutions The ivory tower, the credentialed expert Speech control and thought control The Precautionary Principle Utopia, the “unconstrained vision” Deceleration, de-growth, depopulation Nietzsche’s “Last Man”

The move worth noticing

Sitting alongside corruption and authoritarianism are things most people would call ordinary caution: “existential risk,” “sustainability,” “tech ethics,” “risk management,” “trust and safety.” The manifesto lists them together as one “mass demoralization campaign” running for six decades.

Grouping them means an objection about, say, AI safety arrives pre-sorted: filed alongside central planning before anyone has answered it on its own terms.

The sharpest example

The Precautionary Principle — the idea that you should be able to show something is safe before deploying it widely — is called “deeply immoral” and blamed for stalling civilian nuclear power, “perhaps the most catastrophic mistake in Western society in my lifetime.”

That’s a real and debatable position about a real policy. It is also the clearest case of the manifesto’s pattern: a big claim about the world, delivered without the evidence you would need to check it.

Making up your own mind

Where it’s solid. Where it asks for faith.

Agreeing or disagreeing with a manifesto as a whole gets you very little. It’s a stack of separate claims, and some are far better supported than others.

Hard to argue with

The historical core

  • Life did get dramatically better. The opening point — that for 290,000 years almost nothing changed, then living standards took off — is uncontroversial economic history.
  • Technology drove much of it. That productivity growth, powered by new tools and knowledge, is central to rising living standards is mainstream economics.
  • Central planning has a bad record. The Hayek point about dispersed knowledge is a serious argument with serious evidence behind it.
  • Energy poverty is real. The gap in energy use between rich and poor countries is enormous, and closing it downward would cost lives.
Take with care

The leaps

  • From “often” to “always.” Technology has solved many problems. The manifesto says it can solve every physical problem there is. Those are two very different claims.
  • Big numbers hide people. “Wages rise with productivity” can be true for a whole country while a particular town, trade or decade goes badly. The document only ever looks at the big picture.
  • The one-way ledger. Deaths from delaying AI are counted. Harms from deploying it fast stay off the scale entirely.
  • Opponents get a diagnosis. Critics are said to suffer from “ressentiment.” Naming the motive behind a criticism leaves the criticism itself standing.
  • The author has a stake. A venture capitalist arguing that accelerating technology is a moral duty is arguing for the conditions his firm invests in. That doesn’t make it false. It’s just context worth holding.
Instead of citations

56 names, no footnotes.

The manifesto ends with a list it calls the “Patron Saints of Techno-Optimism,” offered explicitly “in lieu of detailed endnotes and citations.”

Read them and “you too will become a Techno-Optimist.” It’s a reading list where a bibliography would go — and a fair summary of the tradition the document draws on.

Among them: two anonymous X accounts, a fictional character from Atlas Shrugged (John Galt), and the author of the 1909 Futurist Manifesto (Marinetti), whose language the document borrows directly.

Where it leaves you

“It’s time to build.”

The last section asks one question: what world are we building for our children — “a world of fear, guilt, and resentment? Or a world of ambition, abundance, and adventure?”

It’s a good question, posed as a choice between two options. Most real arguments about technology happen in between: which things to build, how fast, who carries the cost while the benefits arrive, and who decides.

You don’t have to pick a team to read this well. You can accept the historical core, reject the one-way ledger on AI, and still be curious about a thousand nuclear reactors. Take it claim by claim.

Read it yourself
It’s short.
About 20 minutes.

The manifesto is a fast, deliberately forceful read — which is exactly why it’s worth reading with the questions on this page in hand. Notice where it shows you something, and where it simply tells you.
