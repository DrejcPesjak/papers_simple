The 30-second version

The paper’s main idea: help people use AI to do more valuable work. Income support, training, taxes, and worker protections can help with the transition. But no single approach—including AI tools—guarantees that everyone keeps a job.

01 The shift

Tasks change first.
Jobs can follow.

Imagine an office worker who spends part of the day writing routine reports. If AI speeds that up, the employer has a choice about what happens next.

Displacement

Technology takes over enough work that a person’s job disappears, or fewer people are hired to do it.

Augmentation

A person uses technology to do their job better. Think of AI as a helper for some tasks, with a human checking the work.

02 The options

Six ideas.
Six real trade-offs.

The paper reviews these responses and argues that each has limits. Open any idea for the basic promise and the paper’s main concern.

01Give people an income floorProtect living standards+

The ideaProvide regular cash payments to everyone, or top up low incomes. Temporary support can also cushion a pay cut after losing a job.

The paper’s concernLarge payments need lasting funding. Extra spending could push up prices where essentials are scarce, such as housing. Cash helps pay bills, but doesn’t itself create jobs.

02Tax the AI winnersShare the gains+

The ideaUse taxes on AI-related profits or automation to help fund income support, training, and public services.

The paper’s concernGlobal firms can move profits or investment. Slovenia acting alone has limited influence. Cooperation across the EU could help, but the amount of money raised is uncertain.

03Train people for new jobsHelp workers move+

The ideaOffer courses, apprenticeships, and support so workers can switch roles or learn skills employers need.

The paper’s concernLearning takes time, and the target job may change too. A completed course doesn’t guarantee a local opening. Training needs to connect to real work.

04Share the remaining workReduce working hours+

The ideaShorten the workweek or split jobs, so more people can share the available work and the benefits of higher productivity.

The paper’s concernIf pay falls with hours, bills become harder to cover. Keeping pay unchanged needs enough productivity or funding. Shorter hours don’t automatically lead to more hiring.

05Steer or slow AI developmentShape the technology+

The ideaEncourage tools that help humans, limit uses that replace them, or try to pause advanced AI development.

The paper’s concernA worldwide pause would be hard to coordinate when others benefit from moving ahead. The paper also acknowledges that funding tools that assist people is more feasible than stopping AI globally.

06Give workers a stronger voiceMake change fairer+

The ideaUse worker representation, notice periods, retraining rights, and compensation to negotiate how automation enters a workplace.

The paper’s concernNegotiations and laws may lag behind technology. Protections can improve the transition, but cannot by themselves create enough new work to replace lost roles.

A limit isn’t a reason to discard an idea. These policies serve different purposes and can work together. The paper’s objections are arguments about policy design, not proof that every version fails.

03 The core idea

Put the tool
in the worker’s hands.

The paper calls this “AI-human augmentation.” The aim is to improve someone’s ability to do useful work as the tools improve.

A first draft of a lesson

The person contributes

Knowing the student

Check the material, notice who is struggling, and decide how to help each student learn.

The skill is more than asking AI a question. It’s knowing what to ask, spotting mistakes, and deciding what to do next.

The catch

These are possible workflows, not promises of job security. Even a team using AI may need fewer people. Higher output also doesn’t guarantee higher wages: demand, employer choices, and workers’ bargaining power still matter.

04 Slovenia

A small country.
A practical place to start.

The paper proposes making AI access and skills widely available. Here’s what that could look like in everyday terms.

Why the local angle?

Adapt where
you have influence.

The paper describes Slovenia as a small economy connected to larger markets. It cannot set the pace of global AI development on its own.

Its opportunity, the paper argues, is to help schools, employers, and workers adopt useful tools together. Smaller firms may especially need shared support.

  1. Make access ordinary

    Let libraries, schools, and job centres offer access to AI tools, so using them isn’t limited to people who can afford subscriptions.

  2. Teach it through actual work

    Use short, practical sessions built around real tasks. Include how to check answers and when human judgment is needed.

  3. Help employers redesign roles

    Give workers time and support to try useful tools. Plan what they can do with the time saved, rather than assuming a subscription is enough.

  4. Keep support around the change

    Combine tool access with training, income support, and worker protections. People who lose a job still need help.

A useful test of the plan

Look beyond how many people use AI. Ask whether work quality, pay, and job opportunities improve—and who is being left behind.

Take this with you

Better tools are a start.
Better outcomes take choices.

The paper’s strongest practical message is to equip people early. Whether that protects jobs depends on what employers, workers, and governments do with the time and value AI creates.

What is argument, and what is illustration?

The source argues that augmentation is the only robust core strategy. That is its position, not an established guarantee. This guide keeps the central argument while making its limits explicit.

The workday hours and profession examples are illustrations, not study results or forecasts. The suggested outcome checks are an editorial addition. The source’s numerical claims, historical examples, and cost comparisons are not independently verified here and are not used as headline evidence.
