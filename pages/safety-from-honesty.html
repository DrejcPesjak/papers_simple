An AI with
nothing to win.

What if an AI could understand the world
without trying to steer it?

Understanding a goal“I can explain what you want.”

arXiv:2606.29657v2 ↗

The proposal in one breath: train an AI to explain evidence, give it no reward for shaping the world, and check its answers before release. The paper makes a conditional safety argument for this design.

A proposal, not a product test.

01
First, separate knowing from wanting

A commentator
isn’t a player.

Imagine two people watching the same chessboard. Both can see the winning move. Only one is trying to win the game.

The player: “Which move gets me a win?”

The commentator: “Which move is likely to win?”

The authors want a Predictor that works more like the commentator. It could understand planning, deception, and even the effects of its own answers without selecting answers to achieve an outcome.

An agent chooses actions to pursue a goal. That can create pressure to gain control or resist being stopped. Scientist AI tries to avoid building those drives into the Predictor.

“Disinterested” means having no stake in the outcome. It describes the intended training setup, not the AI’s feelings or its willingness to help.

02
Design choice / What goes in

Keep the source
attached to the sentence.

Human text contains facts, wishes, lies, and instructions. Learning to imitate all of it can also mean copying people’s goals.

The proposal makes a distinction: someone making a claim is evidence; the claim itself may be false. A goal expressed in text is something to explain, rather than a goal to inherit.

The system also considers what is actually true behind a claim. If three people report rain, did it rain—or did all three copy the same mistaken source?

Bayesian posterior, in everyday words
Probabilities updated using evidence and starting assumptions. This is the Predictor’s target. “Honest” means trying to match it, including its uncertainty. An honest answer can still be wrong.
03
Design choice / What earns credit

Don’t reward the forecast
for changing the weather.

An answer can change what people do. The key rule is that these effects must not become a reason to reward, rank, or select the Predictor during training.

The model can predict an answer’s consequences. It must not be trained to prefer an answer because of those consequences.

A tiny thought experiment

School picnic forecast: the evidence supports a 60% chance of rain. The school wants a big turnout.

“There’s a 60% chance of rain.”

Credit for matching the evidence. People might stay home. That effect is not a training reward or penalty.

Made-up scenario and probability. The alternative illustrates an incentive, not inevitable behavior or an experimental result.

This rule also covers choosing model versions based on their deployed effects. Later observations can become contextualized evidence for a future training run; they must not become a reward for steering the world.

04
Design choice / What gets out

Honesty isn’t a reason
to release every answer.

Even a true answer can reveal dangerous information. So a separate guardrail checks whether a candidate answer should be released.

The guardrail asks the Predictor

How would this answer
change the risk of a specified harm?

Release this answer

vs.

Withhold the answer

Withhold it if the estimated increase exceeds the chosen limit. Uncertain estimates need conservative margins and may call for abstaining.

The surrounding software—the scaffold—organizes requests and proposed outputs. Any planning or action belongs in explicit, auditable software around the Predictor, with its own safety analysis.

!

Small risks can add up. Many individually acceptable answers could together cause harm. Checking each answer does not, on its own, guarantee that the whole deployment is safe.

05
Now, follow the logic

Where does the safety
argument come from?

The paper studies a particular danger: a Predictor whose answers systematically get harmful outcomes past the guardrail.

For the threats considered, this takes coordinated underestimates across related checks—or an underestimate large enough to enable serious harm.

  1. If

    Dangerous patterns are rare to begin with…

    Among possible Predictors with similar training error, only a small fraction must be dangerous. It is not enough for them to be rare across all possible models.

  2. And

    Training doesn’t concentrate on those patterns…

    No outcome reward means no direct signal to select them. But accidental concentration can still happen, so that also needs a bound.

  3. Then

    Training is unlikely to produce a dangerous Predictor.

    This is the conditional result. The proof links the premises to the conclusion; it does not establish that the premises hold in a real system.

See the notation and what it measures

νₜ(dangerous Predictors) ≤ Cbad × Rshell

Rshell is the largest fraction of dangerous Predictors within any band of similar training error, measured under the initialization distribution. Cbad limits how much training can concentrate on that subset within a band. Under the required condition, the bound holds at every training step.

“Dangerous” means exceeding a chosen system-level threshold for excess harm under guarded deployment, relative to withholding outputs. The paper measures the increase in log-odds, not percentage points. Its risk model is the evidence-based posterior, not perfect knowledge of reality. See §§5.2–5.4.

06
The unfinished part of the notebook

The proof has premises.
Reality has to meet them.

Conditional result · Not a safety certification

The authors argue that the two factors in the bound should be small. They have not established that for specific practical threats, and the paper gives no measured real-world safety percentage.

Is the evidence good enough?
A model can honestly reflect incomplete data or bad starting assumptions. The theorem does not show that its target correctly recognizes real hazards.
Are dangerous models really rare?
Rarity must hold within every training-error band, and training must not amplify danger too much. Neither quantity is directly computable in practice.
Can we build it efficiently?
Exact target probabilities are generally impractical to compute. Efficient approximations, reliable uncertainty, and the assumptions about language and learning still need work.
What is outside the guarantee?
Ordinary uncoordinated mistakes, malicious human use, prompt injection, the safety of surrounding agents, and interactions between multiple Predictors are not resolved by this result.

A way to challenge the idea: search for dangerous models among models with similar training error. Finding many would undermine the rarity premise. Failing to find them would not, by itself, certify safety.

Close the notebook. Keep the idea.

An AI can help us see our choices
without having a choice it wants.

That is the ambition. Scientist AI tries to build it into the evidence, the training, and the release of answers. Showing that the safety assumptions hold is the work still ahead.
