One research paper, in plain language

Ten excuses
for ignoring
AI risk

Two AI-safety researchers collected every argument for why AI risk isn’t worth taking seriously, sorted them into ten types, and showed where each one springs a leak. They are not neutral about it — and neither is this page. Here are all ten, in normal words.

AI Risk Skepticism — A Comprehensive Survey
Ambartsoumean & Yampolskiy, 2023 · arXiv:2303.03885

Yampolskiy is a University of Louisville computer scientist and one of the field’s most outspoken voices on superintelligence risk. This survey is his catalogue of the counter-arguments — and his case against them.

Start here

Doubting is fine. Dismissing is the problem.

The paper draws one line before anything else, and the whole thing rests on it. There is a difference between saying "I'm not sure how bad this gets" and saying "there's nothing here, stop talking about it."

Denial

The risks basically don't exist. The upside is so big that talking about downside is noise.

Skepticism

The risks are real but maybe overstated — so let's study them properly before panicking.

Skepticism is healthy science. It asks for evidence and keeps the field honest. Denial just closes the file. The paper's argument is that a lot of what gets presented as careful skepticism is actually the second thing wearing the first thing's clothes — and once you know the ten shapes it comes in, you start noticing them everywhere.

The catalogue

The ten arguments

Each one is a real thing people say. Each one has a specific hole.

The time illusion01
The argument
Superintelligent AI is centuries away. We can deal with it when it shows up.
Why it lands

It feels reasonable because nobody actually knows the timeline, and there's always something more urgent this week.

Where it breaks

The argument quietly assumes the safety work is quick. It might take longer than building the thing — in which case a long runway is a reason to start now, not to wait. And a timeline nobody can predict cuts both ways: it can also arrive early.

The paper's line: this is like buying a fire extinguisher once the house is already burning.

Confusion as comfort02
The argument
We don't even understand intelligence or consciousness yet, so there's nothing useful to say.
Why it lands

True premise. Nobody has a clean definition of intelligence, and the science is genuinely unfinished.

Where it breaks

The conclusion doesn't follow from the premise. "We don't fully understand it" is an argument for researching it, not for ignoring it. Meteorologists don't understand weather perfectly either; they still issue hurricane warnings.

The priority swap03
The argument
Climate change, pandemics, inequality — those are real and here now. AI risk is a distraction.
Why it lands

Every item on that list is real and urgent. The feeling behind this one is genuine.

Where it breaks

It treats attention as a fixed pie where every problem competes for one slot. It isn't. AI safety is a small field, and the same work — making systems do what we actually meant — also fixes today's problems: biased hiring tools, unsafe medical models, systems nobody can audit.

The impossibility illusion04
The argument
That's science fiction. Machines will never do that.
Why it lands

Used to shut down a whole scenario at once: if the thing can't happen, no preparation is needed.

Where it breaks

Almost everything computers now do routinely was called impossible by someone credible. Beating grandmasters at chess and Go, holding a conversation, writing working code, passing exams — all of them were obviously out of reach until they weren't.

There's also a contradiction hiding here: the people building AI sell it on the promise that it will keep surprising us. You can't market the impossible and dismiss it in the same breath.

The off switch05
The argument
It's just a tool. If anything goes wrong, we unplug it.
Why it lands

The most intuitive one on the list. Everything in your house has a power button.

Where it breaks

"Can we keep control of a system smarter than us" is an entire research area — the control problem — and it is not solved. Try unplugging something already copied across a thousand servers, or something people depend on and won't switch off.

And notice the fallback: skeptics often say the danger isn't AI, it's humans misusing AI. Fine — that's still a reason to build safeguards. It doesn't cancel the concern, it restates it.

Faith and surrender06
The argument
It'll work itself out. And if it doesn't, nothing we do would've helped anyway.
Why it lands

Two opposite moods — blind optimism and total resignation — that land on the exact same action: do nothing.

Where it breaks

Hard problems have never solved themselves. Ozone, seatbelts, aviation safety, food inspection: every one took deliberate, unglamorous, expensive work by people who were told they were overreacting.

Assuming the outcome you want will just arrive isn't an argument. It's a wish with the steps removed.

Negative anthropocentrism07
The argument
Machines can't have real goals, creativity, or intent. Those are human things.
Why it lands

This is the paper's own coined term, and it's the subtlest item here. It's a fence: certain traits are declared human-only, so the danger is defined out of existence.

Where it breaks

It's a double error. We assume AI can't have our good qualities, and we assume it will think like us in every other way — which is why "it would never want that" feels convincing.

A system doesn't need feelings to cause damage. It needs a goal and the capability to pursue it. A thermostat doesn't hate you; it still runs the heat until the number says stop.

Who's allowed to speak08
The argument
You don't build these systems, so your opinion doesn't count.
Why it lands

Not an argument about AI at all — an argument about the person making it.

Where it breaks

Even on its own terms it fails. Many of the most credentialed people in the field — Turing Award winners, lab founders, the researchers closest to the frontier — are among the most concerned. On this topic, knowing more tends to raise worry, not lower it.

History as a guarantee09
The argument
People panicked about printing presses, trains, and TV. We adapted every time.
Why it lands

Statistically the strongest-feeling one: doom predictions really do have a bad track record.

Where it breaks

The pattern holds only where the new thing resembles the old thing. Past technologies were tools we pointed; this one makes decisions. Past technologies changed one industry at a time and gave us decades to adjust; this one moves through everything at once.

We also didn't adapt for free. We adapted through labor laws, traffic rules, and nuclear treaties — the exact kind of forethought this argument is being used to avoid.

The grab bag10
The argument
Everything else — vibes, jokes, and one-liners.
Why it lands

The paper's last category collects arguments too loose to sort: mockery, hand-waving, confident claims with nothing under them.

Where it breaks

It's worth its own bucket because of how effective it is. A dismissive joke ends a conversation faster than any real argument, and it never had to be right to work.

The shape underneath

Nine of the ten are the same move

Read them back to back and a pattern shows up. Almost every argument takes a real uncertainty — we don't know when, we don't know how, we don't know if — and converts it into a reason for calm. But uncertainty isn't evidence of safety. It's just uncertainty, and it points both directions.

“Hard to predict” is not the same as “impossible to prepare for.”
Ambartsoumean & Yampolskiy, 2023

What the authors actually want

Not panic. Homework.

The paper isn't predicting robot armies, and it doesn't ask anyone to be afraid. Its conclusion is narrower and more practical than that: unchecked dismissal is itself a risk, because it decides that safeguards aren't worth funding before anyone has checked.

01
Say which argument you're making

Naming it — "that's the time illusion" — turns a mood into a claim that can be checked.

02
Fund the safety work now

It's a fraction of what goes into capability research, and its results are useful either way.

03
Keep the real skeptics

Careful doubt is how science works. The target here is dismissal that skips the doubting part.

The most useful thing to take from this paper isn't a position on AI. It's a habit: next time someone tells you a risk isn't worth thinking about, check which of the ten they just used.
